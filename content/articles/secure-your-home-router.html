Your router decides which devices get onto your network and what reaches them from the internet. Laptops, phones, TVs, doorbells, baby monitors and the smart plug in the kitchen all trust it. Yet in most homes it's the one device nobody has opened since the engineer left.

Attackers know this. Compromised home routers are routinely gathered into botnets, used to redirect traffic to fake websites, or used as a foothold to reach the devices behind them. The good news is that you don't need any networking knowledge to fix the common weaknesses. You need the login details on the sticker, half an hour, and this list.

Before you startFind your router's admin page or app. The address (often 192.168.0.1, 192.168.1.1 or 10.0.0.1) and default login are usually printed on the router. Many internet providers now use an app instead. Settings are named differently by every manufacturer, so look for the closest match.

The checklist

  1. Change the admin password. This is the password for the router's settings page, not your Wi-Fi password. Default admin credentials such as admin/admin or admin/password are published online for virtually every model. Replace it with a long, unique password and save it in your password manager. If your router lets you change the admin username too, do so.
  2. Update the firmware, and keep it updated. Firmware is the router's operating system, and security fixes arrive through it. Look for "Firmware update", "Router update" or "Administration". Turn on automatic updates if offered; otherwise put a monthly reminder in your calendar. If the manufacturer has stopped releasing updates for your model, plan to replace it. An unpatched router is the most common serious problem we see.
  3. Choose the right Wi-Fi security mode. In wireless settings, choose WPA3-Personal if all your devices support it, or WPA2/WPA3 mixed ("transition") mode if some older devices don't. If WPA3 isn't available, use WPA2-Personal with AES. Avoid anything labelled WEP, WPA (without the 2 or 3), or TKIP. These are broken.
  4. Set a strong Wi-Fi passphrase. Length beats complexity: four or five random words (copper-lantern-orbit-mild) are stronger and easier to type on a TV remote than a short jumble of symbols. Aim for at least 15 characters. Changing it means reconnecting every device, so do it once, properly.
  5. Turn off WPS. Wi-Fi Protected Setup lets devices join by pressing a button or entering an eight-digit PIN. The PIN method in particular has a long history of weaknesses, and few people use the feature. Switch it off.
  6. Disable remote management. Sometimes called "Remote administration", "Web access from WAN" or "Remote access". When it's on, the router's admin page can be reached from the internet. Unless you have a specific need, and your provider's app works without it, turn it off.
  7. Review UPnP. Universal Plug and Play lets devices on your network open ports to the internet automatically. Games consoles and some video-calling apps use it, but malware can too. If nothing you rely on breaks, turning it off reduces your exposure. If you game online, you may prefer to leave it on and keep everything else updated.
  8. Create a guest network for visitors and smart devices. Most routers can run a second, separate Wi-Fi network. Put visitors' phones and your smart-home gadgets (cameras, plugs, speakers, appliances) on it, and keep laptops and phones on the main network. If a cheap camera with poor security is compromised, it can't reach the computer where you do your banking. Make sure "allow guests to access the local network" is switched off.
  9. Check what's connected. Look for a "Connected devices", "Clients" or "Attached devices" list. Rename the devices you recognise. If something unexpected is there, change your Wi-Fi passphrase. Repeat this whenever you add a new gadget.
  10. Check the DNS settings, then leave them alone. DNS translates website names into addresses. Some router attacks quietly change the DNS server so that your traffic goes to look-alike sites. In the internet or WAN settings, the DNS should be set to "automatic" (your provider) or to a reputable service you chose deliberately, such as Cloudflare (1.1.1.1), Google (8.8.8.8) or Quad9 (9.9.9.9). If you see addresses you don't recognise and didn't set, reset the router and change the admin password.

Which of these matter most?

If you only have ten minutes, do these four in this order:

PrioritySettingWhy it's urgent
1Admin passwordDefault credentials give anyone on your network full control of the router
2Firmware updatesMost router takeovers exploit known, already-fixed flaws
3Remote management offRemoves the admin page from the public internet
4WPA3 / WPA2-AES + long passphraseStops neighbours and passers-by joining your network

Things that sound important but aren't

Hiding your network name. A hidden SSID is still visible to anyone with basic tools, and it can make your devices advertise the name everywhere they go. It's not worth the hassle.

MAC address filtering. Allowing only listed devices sounds strict, but device identifiers are easy to copy, and modern phones randomise theirs by default. It adds work without adding much protection.

Changing the network name. Using a name that doesn't reveal your router brand or your surname is a minor privacy improvement, and it does no harm. It isn't a security control, though.

Watch for end-of-life routersMany people use a router for a decade. Look up your model on the manufacturer's support site. If it's listed as end-of-life or end-of-support, no more security fixes are coming, and replacing it is the single best upgrade for your home network.

After the router: the rest of the home network

A well-configured router protects the perimeter, but each device still needs its own care. Keep phones, computers and smart gadgets updated, change default passwords on cameras and network storage, and remove devices you no longer use. If you'd like to know whether a compromised gadget might already be on your network, look for the signs in our guide to detecting malware on a phone. The principles carry over to other devices.
