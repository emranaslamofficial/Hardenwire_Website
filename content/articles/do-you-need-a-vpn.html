If you've watched a YouTube video or a podcast in the last few years, you've heard a VPN advert. Many imply that without one, hackers are watching your every move and your identity is up for grabs. It's a profitable message, and it explains why so much VPN "advice" online comes from sites earning a commission on every sign-up.

We don't take affiliate fees, so here's the answer without the sales pitch: a VPN is a useful privacy tool for specific situations, and an unnecessary expense in many others. Whether you need one depends on what you're trying to protect, and from whom.

What a VPN actually does

Normally, your device connects to websites through whatever network you're on (home broadband, a café, a hotel) and then through your internet service provider (ISP). With a VPN, your device first builds an encrypted tunnel to a server run by the VPN company. All your traffic travels inside that tunnel, and leaves onto the internet from the VPN server.

That causes three changes:

  • The local network and your ISP can no longer see where you're going. They see an encrypted connection to a VPN server and nothing more.
  • Websites see the VPN server's IP address instead of yours. This hides your approximate location and makes it harder to link visits by IP.
  • The VPN provider can now see what your ISP used to see. This is the part adverts leave out. You haven't removed the observer. You've chosen a different one.

The core trade-offA VPN doesn't remove trust from the equation. It moves it. Using one makes sense when you trust the VPN provider more than the network or ISP you'd otherwise be exposed to.

Who genuinely benefits

You probably want one if…

  • You regularly use networks you don't trust: hotels, airports, conferences, shared accommodation. See our guide to public Wi-Fi risks for what a VPN does and doesn't add there.
  • You don't want your ISP to see your browsing. In some countries ISPs may log or sell browsing data, or are required to retain it.
  • You're in a country that censors or heavily surveils the internet, keeping local laws and your personal risk in mind.
  • You want to hide your IP address from the sites you visit, for example if you're researching something sensitive or have reason to keep your location private.
  • Your employer requires one to reach work systems. That's a corporate VPN, which is a different product with a different purpose.

You can probably skip it if…

  • You mostly browse at home on your own network with an ISP you're comfortable with.
  • Your main worry is "hackers". Your money and effort are better spent on passkeys, a password manager and updates.
  • You were hoping for anonymity. A VPN alone doesn't provide it.

The problem with free VPNs

Running a VPN costs real money: servers, bandwidth and staff. A service that charges nothing has to pay for itself somehow, and the methods aren't always visible. Researchers and journalists have repeatedly found free VPN apps that log and sell browsing data, inject advertising, request excessive permissions, or fail to encrypt traffic properly. In other words, some free VPNs do the very thing you installed a VPN to prevent.

There are honest exceptions, usually limited free tiers from paid providers, or tools from non-profits. If a free VPN can't clearly explain how it makes money, assume the answer is your data.

How to choose one

  1. Look for independent audits. Reputable providers commission outside security firms to audit their no-logging claims and apps, and publish the results. A no-logs promise without an audit is just marketing.
  2. Check the business model and ownership. You should be able to find out who runs the company and how it earns money.
  3. Prefer modern protocols. WireGuard and OpenVPN are well-studied. Avoid services that rely on PPTP or obscure proprietary protocols.
  4. Make sure it has a kill switch, which blocks traffic if the VPN connection drops so your real IP isn't exposed.
  5. Read the permissions on mobile. A VPN app has no reason to need your contacts or photos.
  6. Ignore "military-grade encryption" claims. Every competent VPN uses standard strong encryption. It's a marketing phrase, not a feature.

Cheaper partial alternatives

If you mainly want to stop your ISP or the local network seeing which sites you visit, encrypted DNS (DNS over HTTPS, often labelled "Use secure DNS" in browser settings) hides your DNS lookups at no cost. It isn't a full VPN, because the network can still see the IP addresses you connect to, but it closes one of the easier leaks. Apple's iCloud+ Private Relay is another option for Safari users. It splits trust between two separate operators, though it only covers some traffic.

The straight answer

A VPN is a privacy tool, not a security cure-all. If you travel, use untrusted networks often, or want privacy from your ISP, a reputable paid VPN is worth the money. If you don't, you'll get far more protection from the free basics: passkeys, two-factor authentication, updates and a few browser settings. And if your worry is how much of you is already out there, a VPN won't touch that. Our digital footprint guide will.
