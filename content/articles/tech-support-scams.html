Tech support scams are among the most damaging frauds that reach ordinary households, and older adults lose the most to them. They work because they turn a moment of panic into a phone call, and the person on the line sounds calm, competent and helpful. The scam doesn't rely on any technical flaw in your computer. It relies on you trusting the wrong person with access to it.

The clearest way to recognise one is to see the whole script. What follows is a composite of the way these scams typically unfold, based on the reports readers send us and on public guidance from consumer protection agencies. The red flags are marked at each stage.

Stage 1: the hook

There are three common openings.

The pop-up

You're browsing, perhaps after clicking a search ad or a link in an email, and the browser suddenly fills the screen. There's a red warning with a Microsoft or Apple logo, sometimes a recorded voice and a loud alarm: "Your computer has been blocked due to suspicious activity. Call Windows Support immediately." The page may stop you closing the tab or moving the mouse away.

Red flag: a phone number on a warningGenuine security warnings from Windows, macOS, your antivirus or your browser never tell you to phone anyone. The pop-up is a web page, not a scan of your computer, and nothing on your device is actually wrong yet.

The cold call

Someone phones, claiming to be from Microsoft, Apple, your internet provider or "the Windows technical department". They say your computer is sending error reports or has been hacked.

The fake invoice

An email confirms a renewal for antivirus software, a streaming service or "Geek Squad protection" you never bought, for several hundred dollars. To cancel, call this number. This one is designed to make you phone them, which makes the call feel legitimate.

Stage 2: "let me connect to your computer"

On the phone, the "technician" is patient and reassuring. To "diagnose the problem", they need you to download a program or open a built-in tool and read them a code. The tools themselves are usually legitimate remote-support software (AnyDesk, TeamViewer, Windows Quick Assist and others) that real IT departments use every day. Here, they hand a stranger full control of your screen.

Red flag: remote access for someone who contacted youNever let anyone connect to your computer unless you contacted them first, through a phone number or website you found yourself. This single rule stops the scam at its most important step.

Stage 3: the "evidence"

Once connected, they show you proof. They open the Windows Event Viewer, which on every normal computer is full of yellow and red warnings, and say each is a hacker or virus. They run a command that produces scrolling text. They may claim your IP address is being used by criminals, or that your bank account is involved.

None of this indicates a problem. It's theatre, and very convincing if you've never seen those screens before.

Stage 4: the payment

Now the fix has a price: a "lifetime support plan", "firewall licence" or "security software", often a few hundred dollars. Increasingly, the scam takes a sharper turn.

The refund twist

The caller says they'll issue a refund, or that you're owed one for a cancelled subscription. They ask you to log in to online banking "to receive it". While they control your screen, they edit the page so it looks as though they accidentally sent you far too much, say $5,000 instead of $500. Distressed, they beg you to send the difference back, by bank transfer, gift cards, or cash at a cryptocurrency ATM. In reality they moved your own money between your accounts, and nothing was ever refunded.

Red flag: unusual payment methodsNo real company asks to be paid in gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers or cash couriered to an address. A request for any of these means it's a scam.

The same playbook, new voices

The script now also arrives with AI help. Scammers use cloned voices, made from a few seconds of audio taken from social media videos, to impersonate a relative in trouble, a bank fraud officer or a company executive. The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report included AI-related scams as a category for the first time. The defence is the same as for tech support scams: hang up and call back on a number you already know. Families can agree a simple code word for genuine emergencies. A cloned voice won't know it.

If you've already been caught

If you've let someone connect, or paid, act now. Embarrassment is understandable, but these schemes are run by organised call centres that do this all day, and quick action limits the damage.

  1. Hang up and disconnect the computer from the internet. Turn off Wi-Fi or unplug the network cable. This cuts the remote session.
  2. Call your bank and card issuers using the number on your card or your bank's official website. Explain that you've been the victim of a remote-access scam. Ask them to stop or reverse payments and to watch your accounts. If gift cards were involved, contact the card company too.
  3. Remove the remote-access software. Uninstall any program the scammer asked you to install. Check the list of installed apps for anything unfamiliar from the same day.
  4. Run a full security scan, using Microsoft Defender or your existing antivirus. If you're not confident the computer is clean, have a reputable local repair service check it, or reset Windows after backing up your files.
  5. Change your passwords from a different device, starting with email and banking. Assume anything typed or saved on that computer during the call may be compromised. Turn on two-factor authentication, and use passkeys where you can.
  6. Watch for identity misuse. If you shared personal details, or the scammer could see documents on your computer, follow our data breach guide, including a credit freeze.
  7. Report it. In the U.S., report to ReportFraud.ftc.gov and the FBI's IC3. In the UK, report to Action Fraud. You can also report the scam to Microsoft.

Beware the follow-up callPeople who've been scammed are often contacted again, by someone claiming to be a lawyer, government agency or "fund recovery service" that can get the money back for an upfront fee. This is another scam. Real law enforcement doesn't charge to recover funds.

Helping someone else

If you look after the technology for parents or grandparents, a short conversation does more than any software. Explain the three rules: pop-ups with phone numbers are fake, Microsoft and Apple don't call you, and never let a caller onto your computer. Put your own number on a sticky note by their computer as the one to call when something looks wrong. Our guide to spotting phishing emails covers the emails that often start the chain.
