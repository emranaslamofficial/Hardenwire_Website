The email usually arrives with a vague subject line, something like "Important information about your account" or "Notice of a security incident". A company you use was breached, and some of your information was "potentially accessed". It's unsettling, partly because the notice tends to say a lot without saying much.

Breaches are now routine enough that most people's data has appeared in several. That doesn't mean ignoring them. It means responding in proportion. Below is a timeline that puts the most important actions first. Don't feel you need to do it all tonight. The first hour covers most of the risk.

First: work out what actually leaked

Everything else depends on this. Read the notice carefully, or check the company's official website (go there directly, not through a link in the email). Breach notices in many jurisdictions are required to list the types of data involved.

You can also check your email addresses on Have I Been Pwned, a long-running free service that lists known breaches containing a given address. It's a useful way to discover old breaches you never heard about.

The first hour

  1. Change the password for the breached account. Do it on the company's real site or app. Make it long and unique, and let a password manager generate it.
  2. Change it everywhere you reused it. This step matters most. Attackers take leaked email-and-password pairs and try them automatically on email providers, shopping sites, streaming services and banks. This is called credential stuffing. If you reused the password, every one of those accounts is at risk.
  3. Turn on two-factor authentication, starting with your email account, then the breached service. Choose the strongest method available. Our 2FA ranking explains the options, and passkeys are better still where offered.
  4. Sign out of other sessions. Most services have a "Sign out of all devices" option. It removes any attacker who got in before you changed the password.
  5. Check your recovery settings. Make sure the recovery email address and phone number are still yours. Attackers sometimes change these quietly to keep access.

"The passwords were hashed" isn't a reason to skip thisHashing makes leaked passwords harder to read, but short or common passwords can still be cracked, and the quality of hashing varies widely between companies. Treat any password exposure as real.

The first day

If financial data leaked

Call your bank or card issuer using the number printed on your card. Ask whether they recommend a replacement card. Many will issue one at no cost. Turn on transaction alerts in your banking app so you see every charge as it happens.

If government ID numbers leaked

In the U.S., freeze your credit at all three nationwide bureaus: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. A freeze stops lenders from viewing your report, which means nobody can open new credit in your name. It has been free by federal law since 2018, doesn't affect your credit score, and you can lift it temporarily whenever you need to apply for credit. You have to contact each bureau separately.

Also consider:

  • An IRS Identity Protection PIN. Any U.S. taxpayer who can verify their identity can get one. It stops someone filing a tax return in your name.
  • A fraud alert. It asks lenders to verify your identity before opening credit. Placing one with any bureau notifies the other two. It's less strong than a freeze but takes a minute.
  • Your mobile carrier's account PIN or number lock, to make it harder to move your phone number in a SIM-swap attack.

Outside the U.S., the details differ. In the UK, for example, you can check your file with the credit reference agencies, and Cifas offers a paid protective registration service. Check your national consumer protection or data protection authority for local guidance.

The first week

  1. Accept the company's free monitoring offer if it makes sense. It costs nothing, but read what you're signing up for and don't give extra information you weren't asked for.
  2. Check your credit reports. In the U.S., AnnualCreditReport.com is the official free source. Look for accounts or enquiries you don't recognise.
  3. Review statements for your bank, cards and any accounts connected to the breached service.
  4. Clean up old accounts. A breach is a good prompt to close services you no longer use, since each is another place your data can leak. Our digital footprint guide walks through this.

The months after: the follow-up scams

Breached data is valuable mainly because it makes later scams more convincing. Expect messages that:

  • Claim to be from the breached company and offer "compensation" or "account verification". These are phishing pages.
  • Call you and quote your real address or the last digits of your card to sound legitimate, then ask for a code "to secure your account".
  • Offer to "remove your data from the dark web" for a fee.

The rule from our phishing guide applies: never act on a link or phone number you were sent. Contact the company through its official website or app. A caller who knows some of your details has not proved who they are, because that is exactly what the breach gave them.

No company will ask for your codeOne-time codes are for you to type into the service, never to read out to someone. Anyone asking you for a code over the phone, by text or by email is attempting account takeover. Hang up.

If your identity has already been misused

If you find accounts you didn't open or tax filings you didn't make, in the U.S. go to IdentityTheft.gov. It builds a personal recovery plan and pre-fills the letters and reports you'll need. Contact the fraud departments of the affected companies, keep a written log of calls with dates and names, and keep your credit frozen.

A breach isn't your fault, and there's no way to make yourself immune to them. What you can control is how much each one costs you. Unique passwords, strong second factors and a credit freeze turn most breaches into a short chore rather than a lasting problem.
