    The Browser Privacy Settings Worth Changing in Chrome, Firefox, Safari and Edge

    Every major browser has privacy controls, but the defaults differ a lot and the useful switches are buried. Here are the settings that make a measurable difference in each, plus a few habits that matter more than any toggle.

    Key takeaways

    • Blocking third-party cookies and turning on tracking protection removes a large share of cross-site tracking. Firefox, Safari and Edge do much of this by default.
    • In Chrome, also review the Ad privacy settings and switch on Enhanced Safe Browsing if you're comfortable with the trade-off.
    • Extensions are the biggest privacy risk most people never check. Remove any you don't actively use.
    • Private or incognito mode only stops your own device remembering the session. It doesn't hide you from websites, your employer or your internet provider.

    Your browser decides what websites and the companies behind them can learn about you: which cookies they can set, whether trackers embedded on one site can follow you to the next, what your device reveals when it connects. Each of the big four browsers takes a different approach, and the defaults reflect the maker's business as much as your interests.

    This guide covers the settings worth changing in Chrome, Firefox, Safari and Edge. Menu names shift between versions, so we name the section to look for. Every browser also has a search box at the top of its settings page that will find any of these quickly.

    Two types of trackingCross-site tracking follows you from site to site, mostly through third-party cookies and embedded scripts. Browser settings are good at stopping this. Account-based tracking happens when you're signed in to Google, Meta, Amazon and similar services. Browser settings barely touch that, but each account's own privacy settings do.

    Settings that apply to every browser

    Before the browser-specific steps, these four apply everywhere and do more than any single toggle.

    1. Audit your extensions

    Extensions can often read and change every page you visit. A shady or abandoned extension, or a legitimate one sold to a new owner, can quietly collect your browsing history or inject ads. Open your extensions page, remove anything you haven't used in the past month, and for the rest, check which sites each one can access. A colour picker doesn't need to "read and change all your data on all websites".

    2. Review site permissions

    Over time, sites collect permission to send notifications, see your location, or use your camera and microphone. In the browser's site settings, reset notifications to "ask" or "don't allow", and remove location access you don't need. Notification spam from scam sites is one of the main routes into fake virus warnings.

    3. Turn on secure DNS

    DNS over HTTPS encrypts the lookups your browser makes to find websites, so the local network can't easily see which sites you're visiting. Look for "Use secure DNS" or "DNS over HTTPS" in privacy or security settings. It's not a VPN (see our VPN explainer), but it closes an easy leak at no cost.

    4. Let the browser update itself

    Browsers patch serious security flaws several times a month. If you see an "Update" or "Restart to update" prompt, act on it.

    Google Chrome

    Chrome is the most used browser, and Google's advertising business shapes its defaults. Third-party cookies are still allowed by default in normal windows, so the first change has the largest effect.

    1. Block third-party cookies. Settings → Privacy and security → Third-party cookies. Choose to block them. You can add exceptions for sites that genuinely need them.
    2. Review Ad privacy. Settings → Privacy and security → Ad privacy. Here you can turn off ad topics, site-suggested ads and ad measurement, which limit how your browsing is used for advertising inside Chrome.
    3. Choose your Safe Browsing level. Privacy and security → Security. "Standard" checks sites against a list of known dangerous pages. "Enhanced" offers stronger, real-time protection against phishing and malware, but sends more browsing data to Google. It's a reasonable choice for people more worried about scams than about Google.
    4. Check your Google Account activity settings. If you sync Chrome with a Google account, visit your account's activity controls to decide whether web and app activity is saved.
    5. Turn off "Allow Chrome sign-in" if you don't want Chrome to sign you in to the browser automatically when you sign in to a Google site.

    Mozilla Firefox

    Firefox is made by the non-profit Mozilla Foundation and blocks a lot by default. Its Enhanced Tracking Protection blocks many known trackers, and Total Cookie Protection keeps each site's cookies in a separate "jar" so they can't follow you around.

    1. Consider "Strict" tracking protection. Settings → Privacy & Security → Enhanced Tracking Protection. Standard is good. Strict blocks more, including some fingerprinting scripts, and occasionally breaks a site. Use the shield icon in the address bar to turn it off for one site if needed.
    2. Turn on "Tell websites not to sell or share my data" (Global Privacy Control) in the same section. In some jurisdictions, including California, websites are legally required to honour this signal.
    3. Review data collection. Lower down the same page, choose whether Firefox sends technical and interaction data to Mozilla.
    4. Enable HTTPS-Only Mode in all windows, so Firefox always tries the encrypted version of a site and warns you if it isn't available.

    Apple Safari

    Safari's Intelligent Tracking Prevention is on by default and blocks most third-party cookies. The settings to check are on the Mac in Safari → Settings → Privacy, and on iPhone in Settings → Apps → Safari.

    1. Confirm "Prevent cross-site tracking" is on. It should be by default.
    2. Turn on "Hide IP address" from trackers. This prevents known trackers from seeing your IP address. With an iCloud+ subscription, Private Relay extends this protection.
    3. Turn on Advanced Tracking and Fingerprinting Protection for all browsing. By default it applies only to private browsing. In Safari's Advanced settings you can apply it everywhere. It also strips known tracking parameters from links.
    4. Check "Fraudulent website warning" is on (in Security on Mac, or the main Safari settings on iPhone).

    Microsoft Edge

    Edge is built on the same engine as Chrome but adds its own tracking prevention, with three levels.

    1. Set tracking prevention to "Strict" or keep "Balanced". Settings → Privacy, search, and services. Balanced is the default and blocks trackers from sites you haven't visited. Strict blocks most trackers everywhere but may break some sites.
    2. Turn off personalisation and advertising options you don't want, in the same area and in your Microsoft account's privacy dashboard.
    3. Keep Microsoft Defender SmartScreen on, which warns about known phishing and malware sites.
    4. Review the sidebar, shopping and other built-in features and switch off any that send data you'd rather not share.

    What private browsing does, and doesn't, do

    Incognito and private windows are useful for one thing: not leaving traces on your device. When you close the window, its history, cookies and form entries are deleted. That's handy on a shared computer or for gift shopping. It doesn't hide your activity from websites, the network you're on, your internet provider, or any account you sign in to.

    Check your work

    The Electronic Frontier Foundation's Cover Your Tracks tool shows how well your browser blocks trackers and how unique its fingerprint appears. Run it before and after making changes. Don't aim for perfection: a browser that's impossible to fingerprint is also one where half the web stops working.

    Browser settings limit tracking from now on. They don't remove what's already been collected about you. For that, see our weekend plan to reduce your digital footprint.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does incognito mode make me anonymous?

    No. Private browsing modes stop the browser saving your history, cookies and form data after you close the window. Websites, your internet provider, your employer or school network, and anyone monitoring the network can still see your activity. If you sign in to an account, that service knows it's you.

    Which browser is the most private?

    Out of the box, Firefox, Safari and Brave block more tracking by default than Chrome or Edge. The gap narrows considerably once you change the settings in this guide. Choose a browser you'll keep updated and are comfortable using, then configure it.

    Should I accept cookie banners?

    Where a banner offers 'Reject all' or 'Necessary only', choosing it limits advertising and analytics cookies on that site. Blocking third-party cookies in your browser settings handles much of the cross-site tracking regardless of what you click.

    Will these settings break websites?

    Most won't. Strict tracking protection or blocking all cookies can occasionally break sign-ins, embedded videos or payment pages. Every browser lets you make an exception for a single site, which is better than weakening protection everywhere.
