    How to Reduce Your Digital Footprint: A Weekend Plan

    You can't erase yourself from the internet, but you can shrink what a stranger, scammer or data broker can find about you. This plan splits the work into four sessions you can finish over a weekend.

    LM
    Lena MarshPrivacy Editor
    Published
    Nov 6, 2026
    Reading time
    5 min

    Key takeaways

    • Start by searching for yourself the way a stranger would. You can't reduce what you haven't seen.
    • People-search sites and data brokers are the main source of home addresses and phone numbers online. Most have opt-out forms.
    • Deleting old accounts removes data that could leak in future breaches.
    • Email aliases and careful sharing stop the footprint growing back.

    Try this: open a private browser window and search for your full name with your town. Then try your phone number. Then your email address. For many people the results are uncomfortable. A people-search site lists their address, age and relatives. There's a forum profile from 2011. A photo appears that they didn't realise was public.

    This information is what scammers use to make a phone call sound legitimate, what stalkers use to find an address, and what attackers use to guess security answers. You can't delete all of it, but you can remove a great deal and stop most of it coming back. The plan below splits the work into four sessions, about two hours each, which fit comfortably into a weekend.

    Session 1 (Saturday morning): see what's out there

    You can't reduce what you haven't looked at. Spend the first session making a list, not deleting anything yet.

    1. Search yourself as a stranger would. In a private window, search your name in quotes, with and without your city. Then search your phone numbers, email addresses and usernames. Check image search too. Note anything that reveals your home address, phone number, workplace, family members or daily routine.
    2. Check breach exposure. Put each email address you use into Have I Been Pwned. Every breached account is a place your data already leaked. If passwords are involved, follow our data breach checklist before anything else.
    3. List your accounts. Search your email inbox for "welcome", "verify your email" and "confirm your account". Your password manager's list of saved logins is another good source. Most people find dozens of forgotten services.

    Make a simple trackerA spreadsheet with four columns (Site, What it shows, Action, Date done) keeps this manageable and gives you a list to recheck in three months.

    Session 2 (Saturday afternoon): data brokers and people-search sites

    Data brokers buy and compile information from public records, purchases, apps and other brokers, then sell it or publish it on people-search sites. They're the main reason a stranger can find your address in seconds.

    1. Start with the sites that appeared in your search. Find each site's opt-out page. Searching "[site name] opt out" usually finds it, and many link it in their footer. Submit the request for every listing that shows you, including old addresses.
    2. Use a separate email address for opt-outs. Many brokers require email confirmation. An alias (see Session 4) avoids handing them your main address.
    3. Use Google's "Results about you". If you have a Google account, this tool lets you request removal of search results that contain your phone number, home address or email, and can alert you when new ones appear. It removes the result from Google Search, not the page itself, but that's where most people look.
    4. Use your legal rights where you have them. In the EU and UK, the GDPR gives you a right to erasure in many circumstances. Several U.S. states have consumer privacy laws with deletion rights, and California now runs a central deletion mechanism for registered data brokers under its Delete Act. Check your state's privacy regulator for what applies to you.

    Be realistic: there are hundreds of brokers, and many re-list people when they refresh their data. Focus on the largest people-search sites and on anything showing your current address. That's where the risk is.

    Session 3 (Sunday morning): close old accounts

    Every forgotten account is a copy of your data that can leak in a future breach, and an old reused password that can be used against you. Work through the list from Session 1.

    Account typeWhat to do
    Services you no longer useDelete the account. JustDeleteMe lists direct deletion links and rates how hard each is
    Shopping sites you use rarelyRemove saved cards and addresses, or delete the account and check out as a guest next time
    Old social media and forum profilesDownload your data if you want it, then delete. If deletion isn't possible, remove the details and photos and make the profile private
    Accounts you keepGive each one a unique password, add two-factor authentication, and remove unnecessary profile details

    Also revoke "Sign in with Google / Apple / Facebook" connections you no longer use. Each provider has a page listing the third-party apps connected to your account.

    Session 4 (Sunday afternoon): lock down what you keep

    Social media

    • Set profiles to private, or at least limit old posts to friends. Most platforms have a bulk option for past posts.
    • Remove your phone number, birthday, school, workplace and hometown from public profile fields. These are exactly what scammers and security questions use.
    • Turn off "find me by phone number or email" settings.
    • Review tagged photos and check-ins. Posting holiday photos after you're home is an easy habit that reveals far less.

    Your phone

    • Review location permissions and set most apps to "While using" or "Never". Precise location is rarely needed.
    • Strip location data from photos before sharing them publicly. Both iPhone and Android let you turn off location in the share sheet, or stop the camera recording it.
    • Reset your advertising identifier or limit ad tracking in privacy settings.

    Email aliases

    Aliases are separate addresses that forward to your real inbox. Use a different one for each shop, newsletter or sign-up, and if one starts receiving spam you know who leaked or sold it, and can switch it off. Apple's Hide My Email (with iCloud+), Firefox Relay, SimpleLogin, DuckDuckGo Email Protection and many password managers offer them.

    Keeping it small

    A digital footprint grows back unless you change a few habits:

    • Use an alias and a password manager for every new sign-up.
    • Decline optional fields: you don't need to give a birthday to buy a lamp.
    • Choose "Reject all" or "Necessary only" on cookie banners, and use the browser privacy settings that block cross-site tracking.
    • Repeat Sessions 1 and 2 every three to six months. It gets much faster the second time.

    None of this makes you invisible, and it doesn't need to. The aim is to stop being the easy target: the person whose address, phone number and mother's maiden name sit on the first page of results. A weekend's work, repeated occasionally, gets most people there.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I completely remove myself from the internet?

    Not realistically. Public records, news reports and other people's posts are largely outside your control, and data brokers re-list people after opt-outs. You can, however, remove a lot of the easy-to-find material, especially your home address and phone number, and slow down how quickly new data accumulates.

    Are paid data removal services worth it?

    They can save time because they submit and repeat opt-out requests for you across many broker sites. They can't remove everything, results vary, and you'll have to share your personal details with the service. If you have more time than money, the manual approach works for the largest sites.

    How long do data broker opt-outs take?

    Anywhere from a few minutes to several weeks, depending on the site. Some confirm by email; some require a phone call. Expect to repeat the process every few months, because records can reappear when brokers refresh their data.

    Does deleting my social media remove my data?

    Deleting an account usually removes your profile and posts from public view, and the company deletes most data after a retention period. Copies may persist in backups, in other people's posts and screenshots, or with third parties that already collected it. Download your data first if you want a copy.
