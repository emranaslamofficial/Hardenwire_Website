    Is Public Wi-Fi Safe? What the Real Risks Are Now

    The coffee-shop hacker stealing your bank password is mostly a story from an older internet. Public Wi-Fi still carries risks, though. They're just different from the ones you've been warned about.

    Key takeaways

    • Nearly all websites and apps now encrypt traffic with HTTPS, so other people on the network can't read your passwords or messages.
    • The real remaining risks are fake hotspots, malicious sign-in portals, and unpatched devices with sharing switched on.
    • Your phone's personal hotspot is usually a safer choice than unknown public Wi-Fi.
    • A VPN adds privacy on untrusted networks, but you don't need one to bank safely on a café network if your devices are up to date.

    For years the advice was simple: never use public Wi-Fi for anything important. Back then it made sense. Much of the web wasn't encrypted, and anyone on the same open network could run free software to capture other people's logins. That world has largely gone. Today almost every site and app encrypts its traffic by default, and the browser warns loudly when one doesn't.

    So is public Wi-Fi safe now? Mostly safer than its reputation, with a few specific traps. Here's what has changed, what hasn't, and the small habits that cover the remaining risk.

    Myth vs reality

    "Anyone on the network can read your passwords"

    Mostly no longer true. When a site uses HTTPS (the padlock, and an address starting https://), everything between your device and the site is encrypted, including passwords, messages and card numbers. Another customer with a laptop sees scrambled data. Google's transparency data shows that the large majority of pages loaded in Chrome now use HTTPS, and banking, email and shopping apps use encrypted connections as standard.

    "Open networks with no password are the dangerous ones"

    Only partly true. A password-protected café network where the password is written on the chalkboard isn't meaningfully more private than an open one: everyone in the room has the same password. What protects you is the encryption between your device and each website, not the Wi-Fi password.

    "You must always use a VPN on public Wi-Fi"

    Helpful, not essential. A VPN encrypts all your traffic to the VPN provider, which hides even the names of the sites you visit from the local network. That's a genuine privacy gain, especially when travelling. But on an updated device, HTTPS already protects the content. Our guide to whether you need a VPN covers who benefits most.

    "Public Wi-Fi is now completely safe"

    Also wrong. The risks have moved away from eavesdropping and towards deception and device exposure.

    The risks that remain

    1. Evil-twin hotspots

    An attacker sets up a network called "Airport Free WiFi" or the café's exact name. Your phone, which has connected to a network with that name before, may join automatically. Now someone else is running your network: they can't break HTTPS, but they can show you fake pages and try to steer you to look-alike sites.

    2. Malicious sign-in portals

    Hotels, airports and trains often show a "captive portal", a page where you accept terms or enter a room number. A fake network can use this page to ask for things a real portal shouldn't: your email password, a social media login, or for you to "install a security certificate" or a connection app.

    Never do this on a hotspotDon't install profiles, certificates, apps or "network tools" because a Wi-Fi sign-in page tells you to. A real hotspot only ever needs you to accept terms, and sometimes to enter a room number, voucher or email address.

    3. Your device is visible to everyone else

    On a shared network, other devices can see yours. If file sharing, network discovery or a media server is switched on, or if your laptop is missing security updates, you're exposed to anyone in the room. This is why operating systems ask whether a new network is "public" or "private".

    4. Domain-level snooping

    Even with HTTPS, the network operator can usually see the names of the sites you connect to (that you visited a health site, though not which page). For most people in a café that's a minor issue. It matters more on networks you have particular reason not to trust.

    Six habits that cover nearly all of it

    1. Prefer your phone's hotspot. Mobile data is encrypted between your phone and the carrier, and tethering your laptop to your own phone avoids shared networks entirely. If you have the data allowance, it's usually the better option.
    2. Turn off auto-join for public networks. On iPhone and Android, open the saved network and switch off "Auto-Join" or "Auto-reconnect". Forget networks you won't use again. This defeats most evil twins.
    3. Confirm the network name with staff. If there are two similar networks, ask which is real.
    4. Mark the network as public. On Windows, set the network profile to Public. On a Mac, keep the firewall on and turn off sharing services you don't need. This hides your device from others on the network.
    5. Go to important sites yourself. Use the official app or type the address you know. Don't follow links from a hotspot page into your bank or email.
    6. Keep everything updated. An up-to-date browser and operating system make the remaining risks much harder to exploit.

    When extra caution is justified

    The calculation changes if you're a likely target (a journalist, an activist, someone handling sensitive business information), if you're in a country with heavy network surveillance, or if you need to reach something that isn't encrypted, such as an old internal work system. In those cases, use a reputable VPN or your organisation's VPN on every untrusted network, or stick to mobile data.

    SituationReasonable choice
    Browsing news, maps or social media in a caféPublic Wi-Fi is fine on an updated device
    Banking or shopping through the official appFine. The app's connection is encrypted
    Hotel Wi-Fi on a long tripFine, and a VPN adds privacy from the hotel network
    Accessing work systemsUse your employer's VPN as their policy requires
    High-risk work or a high-surveillance countryMobile data or a trustworthy VPN at all times

    The bigger picture

    The things most likely to go wrong for you today don't depend much on which network you're on. Phishing messages, reused passwords and missing updates cause far more account takeovers than café eavesdroppers. If you've worked through this list, the next most useful step is making your accounts resistant to phishing with passkeys and checking your home router, the network you use most of all.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is it safe to do online banking on public Wi-Fi?

    On an up-to-date device using the bank's official app, or a website showing the correct address with HTTPS, the connection is encrypted end to end, and other people on the network can't read it. The bigger risk is being lured to a fake login page, so open the app or type the address yourself rather than following prompts from the hotspot.

    Can someone see my browsing history on public Wi-Fi?

    They can't see the pages or content you view on HTTPS sites. The network operator may still see which domains you connect to (for example, that you visited a news site, but not which articles). A VPN or encrypted DNS hides even that from the local network.

    What is an evil twin hotspot?

    A fake Wi-Fi network set up with a believable name, such as the café's name or 'Airport_Free_WiFi', so that people connect to it instead of the real one. The attacker controls the network and can show fake sign-in pages or try to steer you to look-alike sites.

    Should I turn off Wi-Fi when I'm not using it?

    It isn't essential, but turning off auto-join for public networks is worth doing. It stops your phone silently connecting to any network with a familiar name, which is the main way evil-twin hotspots catch people.
